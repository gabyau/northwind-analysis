Discounts and deadlines at Northwind Traders

Three questions for a manager about where Northwind is giving away money and where it is letting customers down: who discounts the most, whether discounts pay for themselves, and which orders ship late.

830 orders from 89 customers, July 1996 to May 1998 · $1.27M in sales after discounts

+$5,680 extra discount from one rep, Robert King, beyond what the same customers usually get
78 vs. 94 units bought vs. needed to break even at 25% off; only ~5% discounts pay for themselves
18% of 14-day orders ship late, because every order takes about a week regardless of deadline

Question 1

Are some reps giving away more discount than their customers would otherwise get?

Why it matters

Northwind gave away $88,666 in discounts, 6.5% of sales before discount. Reps don't own territories: a typical customer is served by 5 different reps, so the same customer can get a different price depending on who takes the order. If one rep discounts more than everyone else, that is a coaching or approval issue, not a market one.

Approach

For the 54 customers served by at least two reps who ever received a discount, each rep's discount rate with that customer is compared with the customer's overall rate. The gap is turned into dollars using the rep's sales to that customer, then added up per rep. Comparing reps on the same customers means a rep isn't penalized for simply having bigger or more price-sensitive accounts.

Main finding

Robert King gave $5,680 more discount than the same customers usually get, almost three times the next rep (Margaret Peacock, +$1,944). He discounted more than usual for 19 of his 33 customers. 4 of 9 reps gave more than the norm; the rest gave less, down to −$3,375.

Business implication

Excess discounting is concentrated in one rep, not spread across the team. A targeted step, such as reviewing Robert's discounts and requiring manager approval above a set level, deals with most of it without changing policy for everyone. Question 2 shows why it matters: discounts of this size don't bring in enough extra sales to pay for themselves.

Question 2

Do customers buy more when they get a discount?

Why it matters

380 of 830 orders (46%) carried a discount. A discount only pays off if customers buy enough extra to make up for the lower price: at 10% off they need to buy about 11% more just to bring in the same money.

Approach

Each customer is compared with themselves. For the 54 customers with both full-price and discounted orders, their average units on discounted orders at each discount size are set against their usual full-price order. The black mark is the units needed to bring in the same money: usual units ÷ (1 − discount). Comparing all discounted orders with all full-price orders would mislead, because discounts tend to go to customers who already place large orders.

Main finding

Customers buy only slightly more when discounted: 4% more units at about 5%, 4% at about 10%, 0.5% at 15–20%, and 10% at about 25%. A 5% discount roughly breaks even (64 units bought vs. 64 needed). Deeper discounts fall short, and the gap grows with the discount: at 25% off, customers would need 94 units and bought 78.

Business implication

Keep routine discounts at about 5% or less, and require a reason, such as clearing stock or winning a new account, for anything deeper. Because discounts weren't handed out at random, a simple test would confirm this before changing policy: offer similar customers 5% or 10% at random for a quarter and compare what they buy.

Question 3

Are orders shipping before the customer's deadline?

Why it matters

Customers give a deadline with each order: 14, 28, or 42 days. A late shipment puts the customer relationship at risk. Knowing which orders run late tells operations where to focus.

Approach

For every shipped order (809), the number of days from order to shipment, grouped by the deadline the customer gave. The buckets (1–14, 15–28, 29+ days) line up with the deadlines, and the black line on each bar marks the deadline: anything to its right shipped late. "Late" means shipped after the required date; delivery dates aren't recorded.

Main finding

37 of 809 orders (4.6%) shipped late, by a median of 6 days. Orders take about a week to ship no matter how soon they're due (median 7.5, 7, and 7 days). So the short deadline is where it breaks: 12 of 68 14-day orders were late (18%), against 25 of 682 for 28 days and 0 of 59 for 42 days.

Business implication

The warehouse doesn't prioritize rush orders. Flagging 14-day orders when they come in and moving them up the queue would address about a third of all late shipments (12 of 37). The other 25, all with 28-day deadlines, took 29 days or more: a slow tail worth investigating order by order.

Data and methods

How this was done

  • Data: the Northwind database (8 tables: orders, order lines, customers, employees, products, categories, suppliers, shippers), July 1996 to May 1998. Every key between tables was checked; no order line points to a missing order or product.
  • Revenue is unit price × quantity × (1 − discount) on each order line. There is no cost data, so results are about revenue, not profit.
  • Comparisons are within customer wherever possible (Questions 1 and 2), so results reflect what reps and discounts do, not which customers they happen to involve. Discounts were not assigned at random, so these are strong patterns rather than proof of cause.
  • Late means shipped after the required date; delivery dates aren't recorded. 21 orders hadn't shipped by the end of the data, and none were yet past their deadline.
  • All numbers and charts on this page are generated by Python code that anyone can rerun from the raw data: GitHub repository (link to be added).
